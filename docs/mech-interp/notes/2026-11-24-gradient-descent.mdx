# gradient descent, how neural networks learn

## part 2: how does a network learn?
[3blue1brown, neural networks chapter 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IHZwWFHWa-w&list=PLZHQObOWTQDNU6R1_67000Dx_ZCJB-3pi)

### terms
- **training data**: lots of images, each with a **label** (the right answer). mnist has tens of thousands of labeled handwritten digits.
- **cost (loss) function** $C$: one number that says how *bad* the network is. low cost means good.
- **gradient** $\nabla C$: the direction that makes the cost go *up* fastest. its size says how steep it is.
- **gradient descent**: repeatedly take a small step in the direction $-\nabla C$, downhill.
- **learning** = minimizing the cost function.
- **test data**: images the network never saw during training. it's used to check that the network *generalizes* instead of memorizing.

### the cost of one example
compare the network's 10 outputs to what they *should* be: 1 for the right digit and 0 for the rest. then add up the squared differences:

$$
C_0 = \sum_{j=0}^{9} \left(a^{(L)}_j - y_j\right)^2
$$

- $a^{(L)}_j$ is the activation of output neuron $j$. $L$ is the last layer.
- $y_j$ is the desired output: 1 if $j$ is the correct digit, 0 otherwise.
- the cost is small when the network is confident and correct, and large when it's confused or wrong.

### the cost of the whole network
average over all $n$ training examples:

$$
C(w_1, \dots, w_{13002}) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} C_i
$$

the network is a function from 784 pixels to 10 numbers, with 13,002 parameters.
the cost is a function from 13,002 parameters to 1 number: how bad those weights and biases are.

### the intuition: rolling a ball downhill
- with one input, $C(w)$ is a curve. to find a minimum, look at the slope. if it's positive, move left. if it's negative, move right.
- take steps **proportional to the slope**, so steps get smaller near the bottom and you don't overshoot.
- you'll land in a **local minimum**, which isn't necessarily the best possible one. where you end up depends on where you start (random initial weights).
- with 13,002 inputs, it's the same idea in a 13,002-dimensional space. the gradient points uphill, so step the other way.

### one step of gradient descent
stack all the weights and biases into one big vector $\mathbf{W}$:

$$
\mathbf{W} \leftarrow \mathbf{W} - \eta \, \nabla C(\mathbf{W})
$$

- $\eta$ ("eta") is the **learning rate**, meaning how big a step to take.
- $\nabla C$ is a vector with 13,002 entries, one per weight or bias.

### what the gradient tells you
each entry of $-\nabla C$ says two things:
- **sign**: should this weight go up or down?
- **size**: how much does the cost care about this weight? a big entry means nudging it matters a lot.

so the gradient ranks which changes to which weights give you the most bang for your buck.

### why activations are smooth
the cost needs a smooth slope to follow, so neurons have continuous activations (sigmoid) instead of on/off switches, like biological neurons.

### results, and the interesting part for mech interp
- this 784 → 16 → 16 → 10 network gets about **96%** of new test images right (about 98% with some tweaks).
- **but** the hidden neurons don't pick up edges and loops like we hoped in part 1. their weights look almost random.
- feed it random noise and it confidently says it's some digit. it never learned to say "this isn't a digit."
- takeaway: a network can work well without doing what we *assumed* it does. finding out what it actually does is mech interp.
