# getting-started roadmap

This follows Neel Nanda's
[Concrete Steps to Get Started in Transformer Mechanistic Interpretability](https://www.alignmentforum.org/s/yivyHaCAmMJ3CqSyj/p/9ezkEb9oGvEi6WoB3).
His core advice: spend **at least a third of your time writing code**, and don't over-learn prerequisites.
When in doubt, move on and come back when you get stuck.

*Note: the sequence is from 2022.* Neel added a note in 2024 saying the 200 problems are "fairly out of date" and don't cover sparse
autoencoders (SAEs), which are now a big part of the field. They're still good practice problems.

## step 0: python basics
If you're comfortable writing Python functions and using lists and dicts, you're fine.
Do everything in [Google Colab](https://colab.research.google.com/) so you never have to install anything.

## step 1: just enough ml (neel's estimate: 10 to 40 hours)
**Done when:** you can train a small neural network (an MLP) in PyTorch to recognise handwritten digits (MNIST).

What to read from Berkeley courses:

- **CS189 (Intro to Machine Learning) is the useful one.** Focus on these topics:
  - Linear algebra review: vectors, matrices, matrix multiplication, dot products. This is the most important one for mech interp, since everything inside a model is a vector.
  - Eigenvectors and SVD / PCA: used constantly to find "directions" inside a model.
  - Gradient descent: how models learn.
  - Logistic regression and softmax: the last step of every language model.
  - Neural networks and backpropagation.
  - Skip or skim kernels, SVMs, decision trees, and most of the classical statistics.
- **CS188 (Intro to AI) is mostly not relevant.** It covers search, games, and probabilistic reasoning
  (Bayes nets, MDPs). The probability basics help a little, and the RL lectures only matter if you
  pick the "Interpreting Reinforcement Learning" category.
- Optional, very beginner-friendly: 3Blue1Brown's YouTube series on neural networks and on transformers.

## step 2: understand transformers deeply (10 to 20 hours)
**Done when:** you've written your own GPT-2 from a template without copy-pasting.

- [The Illustrated Transformer](https://jalammar.github.io/illustrated-transformer/) (read first)
- [Transformer from scratch tutorial](https://transformerlens-intro.streamlit.app/Transformer_from_scratch) and
  [Neel's video walkthrough](https://neelnanda.io/transformer-tutorial)
- Learn [einops](https://einops.rocks/1-einops-basics/). Neel says skipping it causes "a *lot* of subtle bugs".

## step 3: learn the tools (10 to 20 hours)
**Done when:** you understand about 80% of the tutorial solutions.

- The tutorials Neel links have grown into **ARENA**, the standard mech interp curriculum. Do its
  "Transformer Interpretability" chapter (TransformerLens intro, then induction heads).
- [TransformerLens](https://github.com/neelnanda-io/TransformerLens): the library for looking inside models.
- [TransformerLens main demo](https://www.neelnanda.io/transformer-lens-demo)
- Bonus: load GPT-2 Small in Colab and visualise its attention with [CircuitsVis](https://github.com/alan-cooney/CircuitsVis).

## step 4 (optional): learn the vocabulary (5 to 20 hours)
Skim Neel's [mech interp glossary](https://www.neelnanda.io/glossary) until you roughly know what each section is about.
For the big picture, read [A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2021/framework/index.html)
with its [walkthrough video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KV5gbOmHbjU).

## step 5: pick a problem
Pick something rated **A** (a few days to two weeks) or **B** (several weeks). Good starting categories:

- [Interpreting Algorithmic Problems](https://www.alignmentforum.org/s/yivyHaCAmMJ3CqSyj/p/ejtFsvyhRkMofKAFy): easiest, because there's a known right answer.
- [The Case for Analysing Toy Language Models](https://www.alignmentforum.org/s/yivyHaCAmMJ3CqSyj/p/GWCgZrzWCZCuzGktv): 1 to 4 layer models, small enough to fully understand.

Neel's tips: try a few problems for a day or two each and keep the one you like, use small models,
look for simple explanations first, and treat it like a weekend hackathon. Learning a lot counts as success.
